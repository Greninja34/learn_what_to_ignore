0:50 · Say: Every OCR system does these five jobs. Scan and clean the image, find the layout, detect where the text is, recognise it, then fix likely mistakes with a dictionary or language model. Classic systems fight damage in stage 1, by cleaning the image first. We work only on stage 4, recognition, and teach it to see through damage instead; the other stages stay fixed so their errors never enter our comparison. The images are one receipt run through PaddleOCR 3.7 on our laptop GPU: (1) a simulated phone photo of it, straightened by the document preprocessor (orientation + UVDoc unwarping); (2) layout blocks from PP-DocLayout; (3) line boxes from PP-OCRv6 detection; (4) line crops read by PP-OCRv6 recognition; (5) the text turned into fields with simple rules, plus a check that subtotal + tax = total. Stage 5's rules are ours; PaddleOCR stops at the text. Script: deck/tools/ocr_stage_images.py. Sources: Chacko & Dhanya (IJAIA 2014); Dive into OCR (PaddlePaddle, 2022). Modern systems merge some stages: VLMs read whole pages end to end.

1:00 · Say: Inside the recogniser there are four stages, and every known method is a choice at each one. CTC vs attention, if asked: CTC predicts one label per image column and needs no character alignment; it is fast and good for regular, long lines, but uses little context. Attention decoders predict one character at a time while looking back at the image; more accurate, weaker on very long or very short text. What we use in this project: - Stage 1, transformation: none. Our inputs are already-cropped, mostly straight text lines. - Stage 2, features: a small ViT encoder (ViT-Tiny/Small). This is what self-supervised (MAE) pretraining trains. The supervised baseline uses a small CNN. - Stage 3, sequence: BiLSTM in the CRNN baseline; the transformer decoder in PARSeq-tiny. - Stage 4, prediction: CTC for the baseline (CNN + BiLSTM + CTC); attention (PARSeq) for the main model. Only the encoder's pretraining changes between our models; stages 3 and 4 are fine-tuned with the few labels. Figures: Spatial Transformer (Jaderberg et al., 2015); ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021); a bidirectional LSTM (add the source); CRNN with a CTC layer (Shi et al., 2017); PARSeq (Bautista & Atienza, 2022). Sources: Dive into OCR (PaddlePaddle, 2022), ch. 3 and 5.

1:00 · Say: Labels are expensive: every training line needs a person to type it out. SSL makes the labels from the data itself. Hide most of a line, have the model redraw it, and check against the original. To do that, it has to learn what letters and strokes look like. Then a few labelled lines are enough to teach it to read. LeCun's cake: most of what a model learns comes from the data itself (the cake), a little from labels (the icing), a tiny bit from rewards (the cherry). That's why SSL matters where labels are scarce, like damaged historical documents. Image: screenshot from Welch Labs, "Yann LeCun's Billion Dollar Bet" (7:29); the cake is from LeCun's NIPS 2016 keynote.

0:45 · Say: The family tree of self-supervised learning. Siamese networks (1993) → contrastive methods (SimCLR, MoCo) → non-contrastive (BYOL, Barlow Twins, VICReg) → latent prediction (JEPA). Point to "Pixel Level Generative Models" (top middle): MAE sits on this branch because it rebuilds the input pixels themselves; that is our family. Point to VicReg (bottom row): VICReg is our control. A September 2026 paper showed that for handwriting, rebuilding pixels beats contrastive and JEPA, which is why we chose this branch. Image: screenshot from Welch Labs, "Yann LeCun's Billion Dollar Bet" (1:51).

0:50 · Say: Five kinds of damage. Show-through and capture have clean physical models; fading is only partly modellable and stains only approximately. The formula reads: what you see = the front page + a faint (α), blurred (σ), mirrored copy of the back; we measure α and σ on real pages. It is what we actually do for show-through: it has a known physical form, and real pages tell us its strength. That is the "model + fit" step of our approach. The strips are a synthetic illustration on a printed line; real damaged lines replace them once the data pipeline is in place.

0:50 · Say: Three questions: does it help, what transfers, and can we predict the cost of a damage type before we see it. RQ3 is what makes this more than a leaderboard result. The sketches show what each question measures; they are not results.

Full wording if asked: | H1 | Derived corruptions lower CER on real historical lines at the smallest label budget, compared with both plain MAE and hand-chosen corruptions | The gap is within seed spread, or hand-chosen corruptions match it | | H2 | Transfer shrinks as conditions move away from what was fitted: held-out damage > medium > script | Script transfer equals or beats damage transfer | | H3 | Invariance helps when a damage type leaves the character-carrying pixels alone, and hurts when it overlaps them (ink loss, merged strokes, diacritics): a sign flip between the two groups | One monotone relationship across all families, or no correlation | | H4 (control) | A joint-embedding model (VICReg) trained on the same derived damage loses to derived-corruption MAE, reproducing arXiv 2609.35473 at small scale | VICReg matches or wins, so the reconstruction choice needs rethinking | 0:55 · Say: Each hypothesis says in advance what would prove us wrong. H3 is the interesting one: the same invariance should help for background damage and hurt when the damage hits the letters.

1:00 · Say: SSL for text grew from contrastive methods to masked reconstruction, and in Sept 2026 pixel reconstruction was shown to win for handwriting; but the corruptions are still hand-picked (the survey). Document damage is simulated or removed before OCR (PreP-OCR), never fitted to real pages for pretraining. Mintun and Ericsson show that what a model ignores decides what it is good at, but only on natural images. Nobody has joined these for text: that is our gap. Also relevant: Baloun et al. (IJDAR 2025) compare augmentations by trial for page segmentation; Augraphy (2022) simulates document damage.

0:45 · Say: The six papers this project stands on. Lacuna shows SSL works on historical lines; Ericsson gives us the way to measure invariance; Text-DIAE and PreP-OCR model damage, but by hand; Kišš & Hradiš give us code and the baseline to beat; Garrido-Munoz tells us to rebuild pixels. None of them fits the damage to real pages.

0:45 · Say: LeCun argues that predicting pixels wastes effort on detail that doesn't matter, which is why JEPA predicts features instead. For text that argument flips: the tiny detail, a dot or a stroke, is exactly what decides the letter. The September 2026 HTR paper confirms it: pixel reconstruction keeps per-character information that contrastive and JEPA-style models lose. So we rebuild pixels, and the real question is which damage the model should learn to see through. Image: Welch Labs, "Yann LeCun's $1B Bet Against LLMs [Part 1]" (11:20).

If asked whether damaged historical text is solved: on four sets of Islamicate manuscripts, Google Cloud OCR had 49–71% character error rate; pretraining on unlabelled lines plus only 30 labelled lines brought it to 15–46% (Vogler et al., 2022).

0:45 · Say: Anywhere text is damaged or unusual and labels are scarce: damaged documents are where OCR fails and labels are rarest, so deriving the corruptions from the documents themselves lets a small model learn a new collection from its unlabelled pages. Why it matters beyond archives: for research, every SSL method decides what to ignore, and for text that choice is made by trial; we turn it into a procedure and test when ignoring damage helps and when it erases the letters. What we leave behind: fitted damage models, real lines tagged by damage type, and a tool that measures what a trained model ignores. Beyond OCR, the recipe (measure the nuisance, model it, fit it, check the match) could serve any field where damage is physically modellable.
